A free brief · Oskar May

Sixteen things that predict a bad project.

Not vibes, and not "trust your gut". Specific things clients say and do in the first two conversations that correlate with a project going wrong six weeks later — and what to ask instead, while you can still say no.

Read it once before your next first call. It takes fifteen minutes and saves roughly a week of unpaid work a year.

The pattern is nearly always the same. Nothing goes dramatically wrong. Nobody shouts. The work simply expands, the revisions continue, the invoice arrives late, and you finish the project earning less per hour than you would have at a supermarket — while telling yourself it was good experience.

These are the signals. They are not proof of a bad client. They are reasons to ask one more question before you sign, or to price the risk in.

01They cannot name who approves

High

"I'll need to run it past a few people."

This is the single most expensive sentence in freelancing. "A few people" means nobody, and it means feedback arrives from three directions, contradicts itself, and you mediate it unpaid.

Ask: "Who has final sign-off on this? If two people disagree, whose call is it?" Get a name. If they cannot give one, that is your answer.

02The brief is a feeling, not a document

High

"You'll know it when you see it."

Honest, and it means the revision count is unlimited because the target moves every time you hit it. You are not being hired to make something; you are being hired to make someone feel something they cannot describe.

Ask: "Can you send me two things that already give you that feeling — anything, not just competitors?" If they cannot, quote hourly, not fixed.

03They mention a deadline first and a budget second

High

"We need this live by the 14th."

Urgency before scope is how you get a fixed price for an undefined job with no time to think. The deadline has become the constraint that justifies everything else, including unpaid overtime.

Ask: "What happens on the 14th?" Often the answer is a meeting, not a launch. That single question can buy you three weeks.

04"It's simple" appears in the first email

High

"It's a really simple site, just a few pages."

People who have commissioned work before never say this. They know the simple version still takes a week. The word is a request for a discount disguised as a description.

Ask: "Can you walk me through what 'simple' means — how many pages, and who writes the copy?" Then quote from that, in writing.

05They have been through three freelancers

High

"Our last designer just disappeared."

Sometimes true and unlucky. Often the story omits that all three left for the same reason. You are about to be the fourth data point.

Ask: "What happened with the last one — what would you do differently this time?" Listen for whether they blame the person or the process. Blaming the process is a good sign. Blaming everybody is not.

06Payment terms get vaguer as the call goes on

High

"We'll sort out the invoice side later."

The invoice is the part of the project that determines whether it was worth doing, and it is the part they are least interested in discussing. That is information.

Ask: "Deposit on acceptance, balance on delivery, 14 days — does that work?" Say it before the work, not after. Anyone who objects to a deposit is telling you about their cash position.

07"Can you just…"

Medium

"Can you just tweak the logo while you're in there?"

The word "just" is a scope-creep tell. It is never just the logo. It is the logo, then the colours, then the font, then a conversation about the brand.

Ask: "Happy to — I'll price it separately so it doesn't affect the main timeline. Want that now or after delivery?" Yes-and-price beats no.

08They compare you to a much cheaper quote

Medium

"Someone on Fiverr said they'd do it for 200."

Not always a red flag; sometimes it is a test. It becomes one when they expect you to match it, because that means they see the work as a commodity and will swap you for the next cheaper option the moment something goes wrong.

Ask: "What made you approach me as well?" If the answer is quality or judgement, there is a deal. If it is "you were available", there probably is not.

09Every message arrives as a new email thread

Medium

Four separate emails, three subjects, no context.

Disorganised communication at the enquiry stage becomes disorganised feedback at the review stage. You will spend hours reconstructing what was agreed.

Ask: "Shall we keep decisions in one thread so we both have the record?" Then send a summary after every call. Make the record, and make it theirs.

10They want to "see something first"

Medium

"Can you mock something up so we can decide?"

Speculative work, requested politely. It is unpaid, it sets the expectation that thinking is free, and the thing you produce will be judged without a brief.

Ask: "I can do a paid discovery session — one hour, fixed fee, you leave with a direction. Or we start properly with a deposit. Which suits?"

11They are "just about to" get funding

Medium

"The investment closes next month, so we'll pay you then."

Your invoice has been placed in a queue behind an event outside your control and outside your visibility. Funding that is "closing next month" has a habit of closing the month after that.

Ask: "Who is paying this invoice, and when is it approved?" If the answer involves a future event, consider a smaller scope paid up front.

12No questions about the work

Medium

They accept your proposal in one line, without asking anything.

Clients who ask nothing at the start ask everything at the end. The questions were always there; they just arrive after the work has been done, when they are far more expensive.

Ask: "What would make you say this was a waste of money?" That question surfaces the real requirements before you begin.

13They are doing you a favour

Low, but corrosive

"This will be great exposure for you."

Exposure does not pay rent. Worse, it establishes that your work has a discounted value in this relationship, which quietly sets the price for everything after.

Ask: "I charge for the work, but I'm happy to discuss a smaller scope that fits your budget." Smaller is fine. Free is not.

14They apologise for their own colleagues

Low, but telling

"Sorry, marketing can be difficult about these things."

They are pre-apologising for a problem they expect you to absorb. You are being warned, kindly, about a conflict that will become yours.

Ask: "Who else will review this, and does everyone agree on what we're doing?" Better to discover the faction now than in week four.

15They want to pay you in future revenue

High

"We can't pay much now, but you'd get 5% of what it earns."

A share of revenue is not payment; it is a bet on somebody else's execution, made with your labour as the stake. It also quietly converts you from supplier to investor, with none of the protections an investor would insist on.

Ask: "What's the fixed fee for the build? I'm happy to discuss a bonus on top if it performs." If there is no fixed fee anywhere in the conversation, you are being asked to work for free with extra steps.

16Nobody has thought about what happens after launch

Medium

"We'll worry about updates later."

Launch day is the start of the support relationship, not the end of the project. Without a stated answer, "later" becomes a stream of small free requests from someone who reasonably believes you are still their developer.

Ask: "After handover, do you want a support arrangement or a clean break?" Both are fine. Having no answer is how a three-week project becomes a two-year obligation that pays nothing.

Scoring it

Count the High flags and the Medium flags separately. They are not equally serious.

  • 0 High, 0–1 Medium: normal project. Proceed as quoted.
  • 1 High: proceed, but fix that one thing in writing first. One is usually an honest gap, not a warning.
  • 2 High: double the deposit and shorten the payment terms. If they refuse both, that is your answer.
  • 3+ High: walk away, or quote hourly with a weekly cap and no fixed delivery promise. Do not take this on fixed-price.

If you found yourself recognising the client you are currently working for — that is why you opened this. It is not too late to put a change order in, and it is much cheaper now than in three weeks.

The one habit that prevents most of this

Send a written confirmation after kickoff: what is included, what is not, who approves, when feedback is due, what happens if the brief changes. Ask them to reply if anything is wrong. Silence means aligned.

That single message is the difference between a project that runs on trust and a project that runs on hope. It takes twenty minutes and it is the highest-return twenty minutes in freelancing.
